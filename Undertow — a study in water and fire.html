Act II

Anatomy of a wave

Leonardo filled page after page with water: eddies, floods, a current that curls the way hair does.

He wrote most of his notes backwards, right to left, as if they were meant to be held up to a mirror. He drew turbulence the way a detective works a room, patiently and from every angle. The sea behind this text is drawn the same way, contour by contour, and your cursor stirs a vortex in it.

He also drew the polyhedra for Luca Pacioli’s 1509 book on the divine proportion, the ratio of 1 to 1.618 sketched in the margins of this page.

Act IV

Ignition

Fire is the oldest technology, and the oldest argument about technology.

It cooks and it burns. It lights the cave and takes the forest. Every generation inherits the same question in a new costume: smother it, or learn to hold it? The board below records how often we have asked, and what happened next.

c. 370 BCE

The written word

Plato records Socrates warning that writing will breed forgetfulness in those who learn it.

1545

Too many books

About a century after Gutenberg, the scholar Conrad Gessner frets that the flood of printed books is overwhelming readers.

1865

The red flag

Britain’s Locomotive Act makes a person on foot, red flag in hand, walk ahead of self-propelled road vehicles. Motor cars are not freed from walking pace until 1896.

1976

The Cambridge pause

Cambridge, Massachusetts halts some recombinant-DNA work at Harvard and MIT. Within months, rules replace the ban, and the field goes on to give us lab-made human insulin.

2020 to 2024

The fold

AI predicts the shapes of proteins at scale. Its creators share the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

In the matter of

The Future v. The Permanent Pause

Filed at 23:59. Hover a black bar to declassify it, or
Opening statement

Opposition to advancing AI research usually arrives dressed as prudence. Look closer and the costume slips. Its central demand, to stop, freeze and wait for certainty, would not make the world safer. It would make it slower, poorer and less able to understand the very risks it fears, while handing the frontier to whoever ignores the rules.

Exhibit A

The pattern

Writing was accused of ruining memory. Printing, of drowning readers. Motor cars were made to crawl behind a man on foot. Each fear held a grain of truth, and each was answered by learning, not abstaining. The record of moral panic is long. The record of panics that deserved to win is remarkably short.

Exhibit B

The unseen victims

A delayed drug, an unbuilt diagnostic tool and an unrun climate model leave no crime scene. The bill for not knowing is paid by people who never learn what they were denied. In 1850 the economist Frédéric Bastiat named this trap: we weigh what is seen and ignore what is not seen.

Exhibit C

Two hundred million folds

Working out the shape of one protein can take years in a lab. An AI system has predicted structures for more than 200 million of them, nearly every protein catalogued by science, and its creators shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. That is what advancement looks like when it works, and why delay carries a cost measured in lives, not headlines.

Exhibit D

The safety paradox

Interpretability, red-teaming, alignment and evaluations, the tools that make AI safer, are themselves AI research. You cannot secure what you refuse to study, or study a system that is never built. Halting research does not remove the risk. It removes the people best placed to understand it.

Exhibit E

The empty room

A moratorium binds only those who honour it. Freezing open, accountable labs does not stop the work. It relocates it to places with fewer audits, fewer published papers and fewer people asking hard questions in public.

Closing argument

Scrutiny, yes. Guardrails, audits and accountability, loudly; that is how medicine and aviation earned public trust, and critics are right that power on this scale demands oversight. But a permanent pause is not caution. It is surrender with good manners.

At the end of the day, curiosity was never the crime.
